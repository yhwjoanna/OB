Three Modes of Interpersonal Communication

ORAL, WRITTEN & NONVERBAL COMMUNICATION
[ 3 MODES ]
Communication involves both the transfer and the understanding of meaning. There are three basic modes of communicating with others: oral, written, and nonverbal.
+[ 01/ORAL ]

Oral communication

  • Synchronous: sender and receiver are present, attentive, and focused at the same time, e.g., face-to-face, video, or phone
  • Asynchronous: the parties need not be present at the same time, e.g., voicemail
  • Forms include conversations and discussions, meetings, and speeches
+[ 02/WRITTEN ]

Written communication

  • Letters, email, instant messaging, blogs, newsletters, and any medium that conveys words or symbols
  • Some leave a lasting record; others are valued for being fast and transient
  • Distributed remote teams can perform well even when they rely almost entirely on written communication
+[ 03/NONVERBAL ]

Nonverbal communication

  • Body movements, vocal intonation, facial expressions, and the physical distance and timing between parties
  • When interacting with clients, coworkers, subordinates, and supervisors, watch their cues as well as one's own nonverbal behavior
  • Contradictory messages invite misunderstanding; all modes of communication should be consistent
Active listening means sensing, processing, and responding to a message; reflective listening validates or restates the message to encourage the speaker to elaborate

Choosing a Method and Channel Richness

CHOOSING A COMMUNICATION METHOD
[ 4 METHODS ]
Channel richness is the amount of information a channel can transmit in one communication episode. Issues involving emotion, conflict, or back-and-forth discussion call for rich channels; messages that many people must receive or that need a record suit written channels.
Low channel richness
  1. Formal reports
  2. Memos, letters
  3. Recorded talks
  4. Email
  5. Online groups
  6. Voicemail
  7. Live speeches
  8. Phone calls
  9. Video calls
  10. Face-to-face
High channel richness
[ 01/MEETING ]

Face-to-face or virtual meetings

  • Setting structure, assumptions, or expectations for a team
  • Discussing conflict, performance goals, or behavioral issues
  • Matters involving others' emotions or feelings that are easily misread
[ 02/PHONE ]

Phone conversations

  • Matters that must be completed or answered within 30 minutes
  • Problems or ideas that require extensive verbal explanation
  • Messages that need careful delivery when impressions cannot be managed in person
[ 03/TEXT ]

Text or instant messaging

  • Quick, noncritical questions that many people could answer
  • Brief updates the team needs to know
  • Checking whether someone is free for a meeting or call
[ 04/EMAIL ]

Email

  • Communicating with many people on the team
  • Confirming expectations or agreements after a meeting
  • Formal approvals, confidential information, procedures to be followed

Three Barriers to Effective Communication

BARRIERS TO EFFECTIVE COMMUNICATION
[ 3 BARRIERS ]
+[ 01/OVERLOAD ]

Information overload

The information to be processed exceeds processing capacity.

  • Turn off notifications; check email at set times
  • Handle important emails individually; batch less important ones
  • Reply all only when everyone needs the reply
+[ 02/APPREHENSION ]

Communication apprehension

Undue tension or anxiety about communicating.

  • Build emotion regulation skills to cope with heightened anxiety
  • For emotion regulation strategies, see the chapter on emotions and moods
+[ 03/CRISIS ]

Communicating in a crisis

In a crisis, how messages are delivered affects whether problems get solved.

  • Deliver messages effectively
  • Work together rather than in isolation
  • Use technology to track the situation and find solutions together
When choosing how to communicate, consider synchronicity first: does the message require everyone to be mentally and physically present at the same time?

Defining Leadership

WHAT IS LEADERSHIP?
[ 4 POINTS ]
[ 01/INFLUENCE ]

Influence

Leadership is the ability to influence a group toward the achievement of a vision or set of goals. Influence may stem from a formal position or from the person.

[ 02/INFORMAL ]

Informal leadership

Nonsanctioned leadership, the ability to influence from outside the formal structure of the organization, is just as important and often more influential than formal authority.

[ 03/MANAGEMENT ]

Management and leadership

Not all leaders are managers, nor are all managers leaders. Management copes with complexity and maintains order; leadership copes with change and sets direction.

[ 04/EFFECTIVENESS ]

Organizational effectiveness

For optimal effectiveness, organizations need strong leadership and strong management; the two complement each other and both are essential.

Trait Theories

TRAIT THEORIES OF LEADERSHIP
[ 3 POINTS ]
+[ 01/DEFINITION ]

Definition

Focus on personal qualities, seeking the personality, social, physical, or intellectual attributes that differentiate leaders from nonleaders. This is the earliest approach to leadership research, rooted in the great man tradition.

+[ 02/FINDINGS ]

Key findings

Extraversion is the most important trait of effective leaders, but it is more strongly related to leader emergence than to leader effectiveness.

+[ 03/OTHERS ]

Other important traits

Conscientiousness and extraversion are both positively related to leader self-efficacy. Most people want to follow a leader who is confident about leading the group in the right direction.

Conclusions on Trait Theories

CONCLUSIONS ON TRAIT THEORIES
[ 2 SIDES ]
+ [ 01/PREDICTION ]

Predictive power

Traits do predict leadership. They are better at predicting leader emergence and the appearance of leadership than at distinguishing effective from ineffective leaders. In other words, traits predict who emerges better than who performs well.

+ [ 02/LIMITS ]

Limitations

Trait theories help predict leadership but cannot fully explain it. This led researchers to behavioral theories to further specify what leaders do. A key implication: if leadership is behavior, it can be trained and learned.

Behavioral Theories

BEHAVIORAL THEORIES OF LEADERSHIP
[ 3 POINTS ]
DefinitionPOINT / 01
Behavioral theories of leadership imply that people can be trained to be leaders, emphasizing that leadership skills can be learned and developed.
Key researchPOINT / 02
The Ohio State studies found two dimensions that substantially accounted for leadership behavior as described by subordinates: initiating structure and consideration.
Cultural differencesPOINT / 03
The GLOBE program suggests that the appropriateness of initiating structure versus consideration varies across countries. In Brazil, for example, U.S. managers need to lead in a team-oriented, participative, and humane way.

Initiating Structure and Consideration

INITIATING STRUCTURE & CONSIDERATION
[ 2 DIMENSIONS ]
+ [ 01/STRUCTURE ]

Initiating structure

INITIATING STRUCTURE

The extent to which a leader defines and structures their own role and those of subordinates in pursuit of goal attainment, including assigning tasks, setting goals, and establishing performance standards. Initiating structure is more strongly related to group and organizational productivity.

+ [ 02/CONSIDERATION ]

Consideration

CONSIDERATION

The extent to which a leader has job relationships characterized by mutual trust, respect for subordinates' ideas, and regard for their feelings, including concern for employee well-being, support, and encouragement. Consideration is more strongly related to follower satisfaction, motivation, and respect for the leader.

Introduction to Contingency Theories

CONTINGENCY THEORIES
[ 3 POINTS ]
+[ 01/CORE ]

Core idea

Different situations call for different leadership styles: style X suits situation A, style Y suits situation B, and style Z suits situation C.

+[ 02/MODELS ]

Main models

These include the Fiedler contingency model, situational leadership theory, path-goal theory, and the leader-participation model. Each defines the situation differently, but all hold that no leadership style fits every situation.

+[ 03/MEANING ]

Theoretical significance

They seek to explain how leadership effectiveness depends on specific situational factors, giving more flexible guidance for leadership practice.

Fiedler Contingency Model

FIEDLER CONTINGENCY MODEL
[ 4 POINTS ]
DefinitionPOINT / 01
Group performance depends on the proper match between the leader's style and the degree to which the situation gives the leader control or influence; an individual's leadership style is assumed to be fixed.
MeasureLPC
The least preferred coworker (LPC) questionnaire measures whether a person is task oriented or relationship oriented.
Situational factors3 FACTORS
Three key situational factors are assessed: leader-member relations, task structure, and position power.
ApplicationAPPLICATION
The combination of these factors indicates which leadership style is most effective in a given situation.

Situational Leadership Theory (SLT)

SITUATIONAL LEADERSHIP THEORY
[ 3 POINTS ]
+[ 01/CORE ]

Core idea

Focuses on the followers. Successful leadership depends on selecting the right style, contingent on the followers' readiness, i.e., their willingness and ability to accomplish a specific task.

+[ 02/STRENGTHS ]

Strengths

Intuitively appealing, it acknowledges the importance of followers and builds on the logic that leaders can compensate for followers' limits in ability and motivation. Telling, selling, participating, or delegating styles are matched to follower readiness.

+[ 03/LIMITS ]

Limitations

Ambiguities and inconsistencies in the model, and problems with the methods used to test it, limit its practical value.

Path-Goal Theory

PATH-GOAL THEORY
[ 4 POINTS ]
[ 01/BASIS ]

Theoretical basis

Draws its key elements from initiating structure and consideration, combined with the expectancy theory of motivation.

[ 02/ROLE ]

Leader's role

Effective leaders help followers clarify the path to their goals and reduce roadblocks along the way so that the goals can be achieved.

[ 03/SITUATION ]

Situational fit

The leader's behavior depends on the complexity of the situation. For example, directive leadership may be more effective when tasks are ambiguous.

[ 04/DIFFERENCES ]

Individual differences

Followers' individual differences are considered; for example, leaders who set goals actively help conscientious followers achieve high performance.

Leader-Member Exchange (LMX) Theory

LEADER-MEMBER EXCHANGE THEORY
[ 3 POINTS ]
+[ 01/CORE ]

Core of the theory

Because of time pressures, leaders establish a special relationship with a small group of followers. These form the in-group, who are trusted, get more attention, and enjoy special privileges; the other followers fall into the out-group.

+[ 02/SELECTION ]

Selection criteria

Leaders choose followers whose demographics, attitudes, and personality are similar to their own, or who are more competent than out-group members; the choice is not random.

+[ 03/EFFECTS ]

Effects

In-group followers receive higher performance ratings, are more satisfied with their supervisor, and are more likely to engage in citizenship behavior; however, differential treatment may harm the overall organizational climate.

Charismatic Leadership

CHARISMATIC LEADERSHIP
[ 4 POINTS ]
[ 01/DEFINITION ]

Definition

Followers attribute heroic or extraordinary leadership abilities to leaders when they observe certain behaviors, and grant these leaders power accordingly.

[ 02/TRAITS ]

Traits

Have a vision, are willing to take risks, are sensitive to follower needs, and display unconventional behaviors.

[ 03/EFFECTS ]

Effects

Recent research in Greece suggests that charismatic leadership can raise followers' organizational identification (commitment) by building a shared group identity among them.

[ 04/LEARNABLE ]

Development

Charisma can be developed through learning and practice, e.g., using an animated voice, eye contact, and facial expressions to reinforce messages and drawing out followers' potential.

Situational Factors in Charismatic Leadership

SITUATIONAL FACTORS
[ 4 FACTORS ]
[ 01/CRISIS ]

Crisis

People are more receptive to charismatic leadership when they sense a crisis, face stress, or fear for their lives. In these situations, charismatic leaders can offer direction and hope.

[ 02/STRESS ]

Stress relief

Charismatic leaders can help reduce employee stress by making work seem more interesting and meaningful.

[ 03/LEVEL ]

Organizational level

Charismatic leadership matters more at upper levels of the organization, but it can also be effective laterally, from a distance or up close.

[ 04/INDIVIDUAL ]

Individual differences

Some people's personalities make them especially susceptible to charismatic leadership, so its effects may vary with follower personality.

The Dark Side of Charismatic Leadership

THE DARK SIDE OF CHARISMA
[ 3 POINTS ]
+[ 01/SELF-INTEREST ]

Self-interest

Many charismatic leaders place personal goals above organizational goals; this self-serving behavior can damage the organization's long-term interests.

+[ 02/NARCISSISM ]

Narcissism

Some narcissistic behaviors are highly correlated with charismatic leadership and may lead leaders to ignore others' needs and opinions. The high pay and leeway organizations give charismatic CEOs may amplify these risks.

+[ 03/BALANCE ]

Balanced view

Overall, charismatic leadership is still effective, but it is not always the answer; its effects must be judged case by case.

Characteristics of Transactional and Transformational Leaders

CHARACTERISTICS
[ 4+4 ]
+ [ 01/TRANSACTIONAL ]

Transactional leaders

  • Contingent reward: contracts exchange of rewards for effort.
  • Management by exception (active): watches and searches for deviations from rules and standards.
  • Management by exception (passive): intervenes only if standards are not met.
  • Laissez-faire: abdicates responsibilities, avoids making decisions.
+ [ 02/TRANSFORMATIONAL ]

Transformational leaders

  • Idealized influence: provides vision and sense of mission, instills pride.
  • Intellectual stimulation: promotes intelligence, rationality, and careful problem solving.
  • Inspirational motivation: communicates high expectations, uses symbols to focus efforts.
  • Individualized consideration: gives personal attention, treats each employee individually.

27

How Transactional and Transformational Leadership Relate

COMPLEMENTARY, NOT OPPOSING
[ 4 POINTS ]
[ 01/COMPLEMENT ]

Complementary

Transactional and transformational leadership should not be viewed as opposing approaches to getting things done; they complement each other.

[ 02/BEST ]

The best leaders

The best leaders are both transactional and transformational; this combination is most effective across different situations.

[ 03/PERFORMANCE ]

Performance gains

Transformational leadership builds on transactional leadership: it produces levels of follower effort and performance beyond what a transactional approach alone would achieve.

[ 04/FULL RANGE ]

Full range of leadership

The full range of leadership model holds that leaders should be able to use different styles flexibly to fit the demands of different situations.

Supplementary research: transformational leadership outperforms contingent reward by only .05

A META-ANALYTIC TEST OF RELATIVE VALIDITY
[ JAP 2004 ]
87 STUDIES/626 rs
Judge and Piccolo's meta-analysis (87 studies, 626 correlations): corrected correlations with overall criteria were .44 for transformational leadership and .39 for contingent reward; controlling for other leader behaviors, transformational fell to .24. For follower job satisfaction and leader job performance, contingent reward was higher.
+[ 01/EFFECT SIZES ]

Ranking of overall validity

  • Transformational .44, contingent reward .39: a gap of only .05
  • Active management by exception .15
  • Passive management by exception −.18; laissez-faire −.37
+[ 02/WHERE CR WINS ]

Criteria where contingent reward is higher

  • Follower job satisfaction: .64 vs. .58
  • Leader job performance: .45 vs. .27
  • The transformational advantage does not hold for every outcome
+[ 03/AUGMENTATION ]

Augmentation effect

  • Net of other behaviors, transformational validity falls from .44 to .24
  • Still significant in 4 of 5 analyses
  • Does not predict leader job performance
+[ 04/CRITIQUE ]

Four conceptual concerns

  • No clear definition; no account of how dimensions combine
  • Concept confounded with its effects
  • Measures fail to replicate the dimensions; the authors urge studying clearly defined aspects instead
The transformational advantage is a matter of degree; rankings shift with the criterion

How transformational leadership works

HOW TRANSFORMATIONAL LEADERSHIP WORKS
[ 4 MECHANISMS ]
[ 01/DECENTRALIZE ]

Decentralization

Extensive delegation spreads responsibility across all levels, building employee autonomy and decision-making capacity, so employees take more initiative.

[ 02/RISK ]

Risk taking

Encourages and supports moderate risk taking, building a culture of innovation and learning in which failure is treated as a cost of learning.

[ 03/LONG-TERM ]

Long-term incentives

Compensation plans are built around long-term results, focusing employees on the organization's long-term development rather than short-term numbers.

[ 04/CREATIVITY ]

Fostering creativity

Positively affects employee creativity, encouraging innovative thinking and new approaches to problems; followers are also more willing to offer ideas.

Authentic leadership

AUTHENTIC LEADERSHIP
[ 4 POINTS ]
[ 01/DEFINITION ]

Definition

Authentic leaders know who they are, know what they believe in and value, and act on those values and beliefs openly and candidly. Authentic leadership focuses on the moral aspects of leadership, a response to recurring corporate scandals.

[ 02/TRUST ]

Building trust

The primary outcome is followers' growing trust in the leader, grounded in the consistency and transparency of the leader's words and actions. Once trust exists, information sharing and cooperation follow.

[ 03/SELF-AWARENESS ]

Self-awareness

Emphasizes leaders' deep understanding of their own strengths, weaknesses, and values, continually revised in light of feedback.

[ 04/ETHICS ]

Moral standards

Authentic leaders hold high moral standards and do not compromise under pressure, so they become moral role models for followers.

Trust and leadership

TRUST AND LEADERSHIP
[ 3 CHARACTERISTICS ]
Trust is a psychological state in which followers are willing to be vulnerable to the leader; it is the foundation of effective leadership. Trustworthy leaders show three characteristics.
+[ 01/INTEGRITY ]

Integrity

INTEGRITY

Honesty and truthfulness. Integrity-based trust does most to reduce the costs of distrust; confidence in the leader's motives, honesty, and character is the core of trust.

+[ 02/BENEVOLENCE ]

Benevolence

BENEVOLENCE

Has followers' interests at heart and cares about their welfare.

+[ 03/ABILITY ]

Ability

ABILITY

Technical and interpersonal knowledge and skills that convince others the leader's direction serves everyone's best interests.

Outcomes of trust: risk taking, information sharing, higher group effectiveness and productivity

Ethical leadership

ETHICAL LEADERSHIP & SOCIALIZED CHARISMA
[ 3 POINTS ]
Ethical leadership: leaders demonstrate normatively appropriate conduct and promote it to followers through two-way communication, reinforcement, and decision making. Ethics and leadership effectiveness are not separate issues: the ethical tone set at the top cascades down the hierarchy.
+[ 01/TONE AT THE TOP ]

Tone from the top

  • Middle managers imitate top executives' ethical standards, which in turn shape the front line
  • Ethical leadership correlates negatively with follower deviance and positively with citizenship behavior
  • Compare abusive supervision later in this chapter: the two are opposite ends of one continuum
+[ 02/SOCIALIZED vs PERSONALIZED ]

Socialized vs. personalized charisma

  • Socialized charismatic leadership: value-centered, conveys other-centered rather than self-centered values, and models them through the leader's own behavior
  • Personalized: charisma used for personal goals and power, the source of the dark side in the previous section
  • The key distinction is not how much charisma, but whom it serves
+[ 03/PRACTICE ]

Practices

  • Build ethical standards into performance appraisal and promotion criteria, not only codes of conduct
  • How an organization handles unethical high performers is its real ethical standard
  • Voice mechanisms (SEC 9 of this chapter) are the infrastructure of ethical leadership: problems surface only when followers dare to speak up
The test of ethical leadership is how high-performing violators are handled, not the length of the code

Inviting voice does not guarantee voice

VOICE SOLICITATION
[ 2 PATHWAYS ]
Voice solicitation, a leader asking followers for input, has long been seen as positive; yet the same invitation can lead down two opposite paths, depending on how followers perceive the leader.
+[ 01/CONCEPT ]

One invitation, two response systems

APPROACH–AVOIDANCE

Approach–avoidance systems theory posits separate systems for opportunities and threats. Perceived leader dominance decides which one solicitation activates. Low dominance signals opportunity, and inspiration drives voice; high dominance signals an ambiguous threat (invitation or loyalty test), and anxiety leads to silence.

  1. Under high dominance the inspiration path shuts down (p = .453); the anxiety path opens.
  2. Leader prestige amplifies only inspiration, not offsetting dominance-induced anxiety.
  3. Dominant leaders who solicit voice must first state their intent clearly.
[ 02/FRAMEWORK ]
Figure 1 Hypothesized model: solicitation works through two mediating paths, anxiety and inspiration; perceived dominance moderates the first stage of both (positively for anxiety, negatively for inspiration).
The more deliberate the invitation, the more it pushes the interaction toward self-protection

Leader feedback seeking has a sweet spot

LEADER FEEDBACK-SEEKING
[ 2 PATHS + 1 MODERATOR ]
+[ 01/MECHANISM ]

Two parallel signals

TWO PARALLEL SIGNALS

Leader feedback seeking sends two signals at once: followers feel valued, so organization-based self-esteem rises and steadily promotes voice; followers feel their performance is under scrutiny, so workplace anxiety rises. Anxiety relates to voice in an inverted U: low to moderate levels sharpen attention and promote voice, but past the turning point resources are depleted and voice is suppressed.

  1. Apparent sincerity is the master switch: higher sincerity amplifies both paths at once; when sincerity is low, neither path moves much.
  2. In the field sample the turning point is about 3.22 on a 5-point scale, slightly above the midpoint: moderate tension helps, chronic strain suppresses.
  3. Implication: choose the frequency and setting of feedback seeking so follower anxiety stays below the turning point.
[ 02/FRAMEWORK ]
Figure 1 Research model: feedback seeking affects voice via organization-based self-esteem and workplace anxiety; ∩ marks the inverted-U anxiety-voice relation; apparent sincerity moderates both first-stage paths.
Sincere asking has the strongest effect and is the easiest to overdo; insincere asking changes almost nothing

After voice, speed and transparency also send signals

RESPONDING TO VOICE
[ 2 LEVERS ]
+[ 01/SPEED ]

Speed of change

RESPONSE SPEED

When voice targets the leader personally, changing too fast is attributed to window dressing: hard changes should take time, and when speed outpaces difficulty, followers read tokenism, not respect. Lower perceived authenticity then reduces future voice intentions (n = 1,676, η² = .06).

Figure 5 Parallel mediation (Study 3): speed of change affects future voice intentions via perceived authenticity and responsiveness; change difficulty determines how much authenticity is lost.

Transparency of adoption criteria

ENDORSEMENT TRANSPARENCY

Explaining publicly why voice was or was not adopted raises voice efficacy, mainly among those with low self-perceived workplace status (b = .46); high-status employees are already confident (b = .08, n.s.). Transparency compensates the less advantaged, turning voice from a status privilege into a learnable skill.

Challenges to Leadership: Attribution and Substitutes

ATTRIBUTION THEORY OF LEADERSHIP ── SUBSTITUTES & NEUTRALIZERS
[ 2 CHALLENGES ]
Two perspectives question the actual role of leadership: attribution theory holds that leadership is people's explanation of outcomes, not their cause; substitutes theory holds that certain individual, job, and organizational characteristics can replace or neutralize the leader's influence.
+[ 01/ATTRIBUTION ]

Attribution Theory of Leadership

  • Leadership is a perception: people attribute intelligence, extraversion, verbal skill, aggressiveness, understanding, and industriousness to those seen as leaders
  • When organizational performance is extremely good or bad, people tend to credit the leader rather than the market or luck
  • People who look like leaders (decisive, persistent, consistent in word and deed) are more readily classified as effective leaders
  • Implication: ratings of leader effectiveness partly reflect impression management; compare impression management in OB-9

Finding and Creating Effective Leaders

SELECTING & TRAINING LEADERS
[ 2 ROUTES ]
Organizations obtain effective leaders in two ways: identify them in selection, or train them after hiring. Evidence on both points to the same thing: what can be trained is specific behavior, not personality.
+[ 01/SELECTING ]

Selection

  • First define the type of leadership the position requires, then match candidates: different situations call for different leaders
  • Personality tests can identify extraversion, conscientiousness, and openness; high self-monitors adapt their behavior to situations better
  • Emotional intelligence (OB-5) is related to charismatic leadership effectiveness
  • Interviews and assessment centers show candidates' actual behavior in simulated situations, more reliably than self-reports
+[ 02/TRAINING ]

Training

  • Training works better for high self-monitors, who can better adapt learned behaviors to the situation
  • Trainable content: trust building, mentoring, situational analysis (analyzing situations, adjusting style)
  • Charismatic behaviors can be taught: articulating a vision, leading by example, conveying high expectations and confidence; training in transformational leadership skills also has supporting evidence
  • Limits of training: limited effect for people lacking basic traits (e.g., low extraversion, low emotional stability)
Selection matches traits to situations; training teaches observable behaviors

Abusive Supervision

ABUSIVE SUPERVISION
[ 4 POINTS ]
[ 01/DEFINITION ]

Definition

Supervisors' sustained hostile, belittling, or humiliating treatment of subordinates in everyday interactions, such as public ridicule, the silent treatment, and deliberate isolation.

[ 02/SCOPE ]

Scope

It involves no physical violence, yet harms subordinates' health, work attitudes, and performance just as deeply.

[ 03/CATEGORY ]

Academic Classification

A form of destructive leadership, associated with subordinates' deviant behavior, turnover intentions, and family conflict.

[ 04/CORE ]

Core Features

Its core is sustained, repeated, negative nonphysical behavior; organizations should set up grievance channels and hold managers accountable.

Supplementary Research: Protégés Don't Ask for Fear of Being a Burden

THE INEQUITY ILLUSION OF ADVICE SEEKING ── BURMEISTER & LEVIN
[ AMJ 2026 ]
9 STUDIES/2 FIELD EXPERIMENTS
Advice seekers often see the exchange as one-way: they get help while the adviser loses time and energy. The paper calls this the inequity illusion of advice seeking, accompanied by burden anxiety. Across nine studies, prompting seekers to first consider what advice givers gain increased advice seeking.
+[ 01/PUZZLE ]

Why Encouragement Alone Falls Short

  • Common advice stresses seeker gains but misses the emotional barrier
  • The illusion deters analysts from asking senior managers and job seekers from informational interviews
  • Higher status and lower familiarity strengthen the illusion and the reframing effect
[ 02/MECHANISM ]

What Advice Givers Gain

  • Feeling useful; having expertise valued
  • Learning something new; valuable connections
  • Reflecting on their own situation
  • Energy from talking with like-minded people
[ 03/PRACTICE ]

What Managers and Systems Can Do

  • Frame advice seeking as mutual value: advisers feel respected, useful, energized
  • Have seekers state why they chose the adviser, signaling recognition of expertise
  • Senior staff share their gains from advising; managers model advice seeking
[ 04/MENTORING ]

Implications for Mentoring

  • Protégés' reluctance to take mentors' time is a common failure point; stating mentor gains in matching materials is the lowest-cost fix
  • Mentors should affirm the protégé's ideal self, not impose their own; in couples, imposed ideals lower relationship quality (the Michelangelo phenomenon)
Effective systems not only provide access to experts but also lower the emotional cost of using that access
