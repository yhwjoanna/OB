Group

WHAT IS A GROUP?
[ FORMAL+INFORMAL ]
Two or more individuals, interacting and interdependent, who come together to achieve particular objectives. Groups can be formal or informal.
+ [ 01/FORMAL ]

Formal group

FORMAL GROUPS

A designated work group defined by the organization's structure, with clearly assigned tasks. The behaviors members should engage in are stipulated by and directed toward organizational goals, e.g., the flight attendants on an aircrew.

+ [ 02/INFORMAL ]

Informal group

INFORMAL GROUPS

Neither formally structured nor organizationally determined. Forms naturally in response to the need for social contact, e.g., coworkers who regularly lunch together, and often strongly shapes members' behavior and performance.

Ingroups and Outgroups

INGROUPS AND OUTGROUPS
[ 3 CONCEPTS ]
+[ 01/INGROUP ]

Ingroup favoritism

INGROUP FAVORITISM

When we see members of our own group (the ingroup) as better than others, we tend to favor our group. This attitude reflects a strong group consciousness and sense of belonging. Ingroup favoritism leads people to overrate their own members' performance and skews resource allocation.

+[ 02/OUTGROUP ]

Outgroup

OUTGROUP

Where there is an ingroup, there is necessarily an outgroup. The outgroup usually consists of those outside our group, often seen as different from or hostile to us. People tend to see outgroup members as all alike, overlooking individual differences.

+[ 03/TENSION ]

Intergroup tension

Ingroups and outgroups often develop dislike or hostility toward each other, which may stem from differences in religion, status, or other factors. Departmental rivalries and entrenched factions within organizations are everyday expressions of ingroup-outgroup dynamics.

Roles and Expectations

ROLES & EXPECTATIONS
[ 3 CONCEPTS ]
+[ 01/ROLE ]

Roles

ROLE

A set of expected behavior patterns attributed to someone occupying a given position in a social unit. Everyone plays multiple roles at once, and the demands of different roles may conflict.

+[ 02/PERCEPTION ]

Role perception

ROLE PERCEPTION

An individual's view of how they are supposed to act in a given situation. Role perceptions are often learned from role models, mentors, and media portrayals.

+[ 03/EXPECTATIONS ]

Role expectations

ROLE EXPECTATIONS

How others believe a person should act in a given situation. When role perception and role expectations diverge too far, role conflict and stress follow.

Norms in Groups

NORMS
[ 5 POINTS ]
DefinitionPOINT / 01
Norms are standards of behavior that group members share and follow.
FunctionPOINT / 02
Norms tell members what they ought and ought not to do: guidelines for behavior in specific situations.
Link to leadershipPOINT / 03
Even when members are willing to accept the leader's views, norms may limit how fully the leader's influence takes effect.
InternalizationPOINT / 04
Once agreed to and accepted by the group, norms influence members' behavior with a minimum of external control.
Positive effectsPOINT / 05
Sound norms help strengthen members' cohesiveness and willingness to cooperate.

Status and Groups

STATUS CHARACTERISTICS THEORY
[ 3 SOURCES ]
+[ 01/SOURCES ]

Status characteristics theory

Status can derive from three sources: power over others, contribution to group goals, and personal characteristics. Status is an informal social ranking that need not match job titles.

+[ 02/DIFFERENCES ]

Status differences

Status differences among members affect their power, influence, and voice in the group. High-status members feel freer to deviate from norms and speak up; low-status members tend to stay silent to protect themselves.

+[ 03/LEADERS ]

Leaders and core members

Hold higher status, gain group resources and support more easily, and shape group decisions. When status gaps are too wide, low-status members' ideas rarely surface, and the group loses creativity.

An individual's status depends not only on their own performance but also on how other members perceive and evaluate them. Balancing status differences is key to keeping the team working

Effects of Group Size on Group Behavior

GROUP SIZE & SOCIAL LOAFING
[ 3 FINDINGS ]
+[ 01/SIZE×TASK ]

Size and task type

Larger groups suit tasks needing diverse input, such as fact-finding; smaller groups are better at doing something with that input. As size grows, individual contributions become less identifiable.

+[ 02/SEVEN ]

About seven is most effective

Social psychology research finds that groups of about seven members tend to be more effective at taking action. With too many members, coordination costs and competition for airtime quickly erode efficiency.

+[ 03/LOAFING ]

Social loafing

SOCIAL LOAFING

Individuals tend to exert less effort when working in a group than when working alone, which reduces group effectiveness. Causes include diffusion of responsibility and equity comparisons in which others seem not to pull their weight.

Preventing social loafing: set group goals, increase intergroup competition, use peer evaluation, select highly motivated members who prefer group work, and base part of rewards on individual contributions

Group Diversity: A Double-Edged Sword

THE DOUBLE-EDGED SWORD OF DIVERSITY
[ 2 SIDES ]
+ [ 01/FAULTLINES ]

Divisions and conflict

The greater the personal differences among members (e.g., gender, age, education), the more likely faultlines form, splitting the group into isolated subgroups, causing misunderstanding and conflict, and lowering group efficiency.

+ [ 02/VALUE ]

Value of diversity

If a group can accommodate members' differences, diversity can foster open thinking and innovation and lead to better performance. Members' different backgrounds and perspectives complement each other and spark more ideas.

Supplementary research: neuroinclusion through universal design, not case-by-case accommodation

NEUROINCLUSION BY UNIVERSAL DESIGN ── SPOOR ET AL.
[ AMLE 2026 ]
CONCEPTUAL ESSAY
Business schools and workplaces assume neurotypical ways of communicating, so neurodivergent people (about 15% to 20% of the population) fit in only by masking. Substantive change requires universal design: building flexibility into processes from the start instead of adding individual accommodations afterward.
+[ 01/PROBLEM ]

The cost of neurotypical defaults

  • Double empathy problem: misunderstanding runs both ways, but only one side carries the burden
  • Masking is linked to stress and burnout; nearly half of 1,100+ managers would not hire neurodivergent people
[ 02/SYMBOLIC vs SUBSTANTIVE ]

Symbolic vs. substantive

  • Symbolic: slogans, awareness campaigns, after-the-fact accommodations
  • Substantive: redesigning selection, onboarding, evaluation, and promotion (in class: UDL)
  • Test: must people still disclose and prove a need before getting an exception?
[ 03/UNIVERSAL DESIGN FOR WORK ]

Universal Design for Work (UDW)

  • Selection: questions in advance, structured interviews, work samples
  • Onboarding: a one-page personal user manual from every team member
  • Meetings: agendas in advance and several ways to take part
[ 04/TAIWAN & ASIA ]

Implications for Taiwanese and Asian firms

  • Quota hiring (1% for employers with 67+ staff) counts heads without changing processes
  • Sustainability reports disclose diversity under GRI 405, but not processes
  • High-context communication widens the double empathy gap
Universal design is tested by participation without disclosure or proof; slogans and individual accommodations alone are what the paper calls neurodiversity lite

Example: Personal User Manual

PERSONAL USER MANUAL ── A WORKED EXAMPLE
[ EXAMPLE ]
FICTIONAL EMPLOYEE
A personal user manual is a one-page note on how a member communicates, focuses, and prefers feedback, shared with the whole team. When everyone writes one, implicit rules become explicit and no one has to disclose first. Below: a fictional data analyst in the first week on the job.
+[ 01/SAMPLE ]

User manual: data analyst, first week on the job

ChannelsNon-urgent: text or email, so I can think first; urgent: phone or in person. Please send agendas before meetings.
Response timeReplies within two hours; 9-11 a.m. is my focus block (urgent messages only).
Learning styleI read examples first and need a step-by-step checklist for a new task; I repeat verbal instructions back once.
FocusOne task at a time, with clear completion criteria; headphones on means I am concentrating.
DistractionsOpen-office chatter, sudden questions from behind, several people assigning work at once.
FeedbackDirect and specific, with examples, in a one-on-one rather than in the group.
Say it directlyI often miss hints, so please state what you need. Skipping after-work dinners is not unfriendliness.
[ 02/HOW TO WRITE ]

Writing guidelines

  • Observable behaviors, not personality adjectives
  • Work matters only; no diagnoses or medical history
  • Say how others can help, not just preferences
  • Write in week one; update every six months
[ 03/HOW TEAMS USE IT ]

How teams use it

  • Manager writes one first and reads it with each newcomer
  • Combine all manuals into a team charter
  • Class activity: write one in 10 minutes, swap in pairs
The manual describes ways of working, not a diagnosis

Strengths and Weaknesses of Group Decision Making

STRENGTHS & WEAKNESSES OF GROUP DECISION MAKING
[ 2 SIDES ]
Group decision making pools more information and perspectives but also brings conformity pressure and diffused responsibility. On effectiveness (accuracy, creativity, acceptance), groups usually do better; on efficiency (time, cost), individuals usually do better. Which to use depends on the task.
+[ 01/STRENGTHS ]

Strengths

  • More complete information and knowledge: more members bring more usable resources
  • Greater diversity of views: heterogeneous members open up more alternatives to consider
  • Usually higher decision quality: most research finds groups outperform the average member
  • Greater acceptance of solutions: those who take part in a decision are more willing to support and implement it
+[ 02/WEAKNESSES ]

Weaknesses

  • Time-consuming: convening and discussion cost more than an individual decision
  • Conformity pressure: dissent is suppressed to maintain agreement
  • Domination by a few: discussion is controlled by one or two members who may not be the most capable
  • Ambiguous responsibility: individual decisions have clear accountability; group decisions spread it across everyone
Effectiveness criteria favor groups, efficiency criteria favor individuals; choose the method based on the task's need for accuracy, creativity, and acceptance

Groupthink and Groupshift

GROUPTHINK & GROUPSHIFT
[ 2 BY-PRODUCTS ]
Two by-products of group decision making: groupthink, in which conformity norms override realistic appraisal of minority or unpopular views; and groupshift, in which the group's position after discussion is more extreme than members' initial leanings, usually toward greater risk.
+[ 01/GROUPTHINK ]

Groupthink

  • Symptoms: rationalizing objections, pressure on doubters, silence treated as consent, illusion of unanimity
  • Conditions: strong group identity, leader states a position early, isolation from outside information, facing a threat
  • Remedies: keep the group to 10 or fewer; leader listens before speaking and encourages all members to speak; appoint a devil's advocate; have members write down views before discussion
+[ 02/GROUPSHIFT ]

Groupshift

  • Pattern: conservative groups become more conservative after discussion, risk-taking groups more risk-taking; the latter is more common
  • Explanation 1: discussion makes members more confident in their initial leanings, so they go further
  • Explanation 2: diffusion of responsibility; no one is solely accountable for the collective decision
  • Managerial implication: knowing members' initial leanings lets managers anticipate the direction of the shift
The common remedy for both is to record individual opinions before the group assimilates them

Three Group Decision-Making Techniques

INTERACTING GROUPS, BRAINSTORMING, NOMINAL GROUP TECHNIQUE
[ 3 TECHNIQUES ]
The three techniques involve trade-offs in number of ideas, quality of ideas, social pressure, and time cost; in most comparative studies the nominal group technique produces the most and best ideas, at the cost of the most formalized process.
+[ 01/INTERACTING ]

Interacting groups

  • Most common: face-to-face, reaching decisions through verbal and nonverbal interaction
  • Strength: direct communication among members; higher cohesiveness and commitment
  • Weakness: conformity pressure and domination by a few are strongest here, a breeding ground for groupthink
+[ 02/BRAINSTORMING ]

Brainstorming

  • Four rules: no criticism, wild ideas welcome, quantity first, build on others' ideas
  • Purpose: overcome the suppression of creativity in interacting groups
  • Limitation: production blocking; only one person can speak at a time, so others forget or abandon their ideas while waiting. Research shows individuals working alone often generate more ideas than groups
+[ 03/NOMINAL GROUP ]

Nominal group technique

  • Steps: members first write down ideas independently; each presents one idea in turn, without discussion; the group discusses and clarifies each idea; members rank ideas silently and independently, and the highest-ranked idea is the decision
  • Strength: members meet together but think independently without restriction
  • Use when: dominance and conformity must be avoided while keeping group discussion
Interacting groups favor cohesion and commitment, brainstorming favors creativity, the nominal group technique favors quantity and quality of ideas

Groups vs. Teams

WORK GROUPS vs. WORK TEAMS
[ SYNERGY ]
+ [ 01/WORK GROUP ]

Work group

WORK GROUP

Mainly shares information; individual members make decisions within their own areas of responsibility, with no need for joint effort, so performance is simply the sum of individual contributions. A group is not necessarily bad, but it does not create synergy.

+ [ 02/WORK TEAM ]

Work team

WORK TEAM

Members coordinate their efforts to generate positive synergy, so team performance exceeds the sum of individual inputs. Individual and mutual accountability coexist, and skills are complementary.

Limited authority

Effectiveness of Self-Managed Teams

EFFECTIVENESS OF SELF-MANAGED TEAMS
[ 3 FACTORS ]
+[ 01/REWARDS ]

Rewards

Effectiveness depends on the extent to which team-promoting behaviors are rewarded. Research on the overall effectiveness of self-managed teams is mixed; situational conditions are key.

+[ 02/CONFLICT ]

Conflict management

Failure to manage conflict can lead to poor performance. Without a supervisor as arbiter, members must negotiate their own disagreements.

+[ 03/GOOD CONFLICT ]

Constructive conflict

Moderate task conflict helps generate perspectives and improve decision quality; the key is to keep it from escalating into personal conflict.

Virtual Teams

VIRTUAL TEAMS
[ TYPE/04 ]

Definition

Use computer technology to unite physically dispersed members to achieve a common goal. Without face-to-face cues, social connection and information sharing are harder.

Team Effectiveness: Composition

TEAM COMPOSITION
[ 3 FACTORS ]
+[ 01/ABILITIES ]

Abilities

Members' knowledge, skills, and abilities are critical to team performance. For complex tasks, the proportion of high-ability members and how they are combined directly set the team's ceiling.

+[ 02/PERSONALITY ]

Personality

Members high in conscientiousness and openness help raise team performance. A single extremely low-conscientiousness or uncooperative member can drag down the whole team.

+[ 03/ROLES ]

Allocation of roles

Select members based on skills and performance to fill different role needs; ensuring every key role is filled matters more than reaching headcount.

Goal Properties

Team Process: Team Identity

TEAM IDENTITY
[ 3 POINTS ]
+[ 01/HOW ]

How to Foster It

Recognizing individual skills and creating a climate of respect and inclusion foster positive team identity. Members who see the team's success or failure as their own become more engaged.

+[ 02/BALANCE ]

Need for Balance

Team identity must be balanced with organizational identity, so that rigid boundaries and silo thinking between teams do not sacrifice the interests of the organization as a whole.

+[ 03/EFFECT ]

Effects

Positive team identity raises team performance and member satisfaction and lowers members' intention to leave the team.

CONFLICT LEVELS
[ 2 TYPES ]
+ [ 01/RELATIONSHIP ]

Relationship Conflict

RELATIONSHIP CONFLICT

Interpersonal incompatibility is almost always harmful to team development: it erodes trust and diverts attention from the task.

+ [ 02/TASK ]

Task Conflict

TASK CONFLICT

Disagreement about the task can stimulate discussion and critical evaluation of alternatives, improving decision quality, provided it stays moderate and does not become personal.

Turning Individuals into Team Players

TURNING INDIVIDUALS INTO TEAM PLAYERS
[ 3 LEVERS ]
Many people grow up in environments that value individual achievement and are used to working alone. Organizations have three levers for turning individuals into team players: selection, training, and rewards.
+[ 01/SELECTING ]

Selection

  • Beyond technical skills, check interpersonal skills and fit with team roles
  • Three options for candidates lacking team skills: train them, transfer them to units without teamwork, or do not hire them
  • Link to this chapter's personal user manual: candidates can be asked to describe their working style during selection
+[ 02/TRAINING ]

Training

  • Workshops on problem solving, communication, negotiation, conflict management, and coaching skills
  • Members experience the typical situations of each stage of team development, reducing misreadings of the storming stage
  • Training matters most in self-managed teams, since no supervisor handles these issues for the team
+[ 03/REWARDING ]

Rewards

  • Change reward systems from encouraging individual competition to encouraging cooperation
  • Reward team behaviors such as training new members, sharing information, helping resolve conflicts, and learning new skills
  • Intrinsic rewards: the challenge and enjoyment of teamwork itself; for the effect of reward structure on idea vetoing, see the supplementary research at the end of this chapter
The three levers must align: if rewards count only individual results, the cooperative focus of selection and training is cancelled out

Teams Are Not Always the Answer

BEWARE: TEAMS ARE NOT ALWAYS THE ANSWER
[ 3 TESTS ]
Communication, conflict handling, and meeting time are all costs of teams; individual work is often more efficient. Before deciding to use a team, apply three tests.
+[ 01/COMPLEXITY ]

Is the Work Complex Enough?

  • Is the work complex enough to require different perspectives and expertise?
  • Simple tasks that need no diverse input are done faster by individuals
+[ 02/COMMON PURPOSE ]

Is There a Common Purpose?

  • Do members share a common purpose that is greater than the sum of individual goals?
  • Putting separate jobs under one name does not make a team
+[ 03/INTERDEPENDENCE ]

Are Members Interdependent?

  • Are the tasks interdependent, so that one person's success depends on the success of others?
  • Without interdependence, the team exists in name only and the risk of social loafing rises
If any of the three tests fails, consider individual work or a work group

Supplementary Research: Individual and Team Rewards Both Raise Idea Vetoing

REWARD STRUCTURES AND IDEA REJECTION IN TEAMS
[ JOB 2026 ]
LAB/80 TEAMS
Lab experiment: purely individual and purely team rewards both raised idea vetoing compared with mixed rewards, and more vetoing meant lower team creativity (b = −0.06, p < .01). The team-reward result runs against theory.
+ [ 01/PUZZLE ]

Why Study Vetoing

Diverse teams generate ideas but may veto unusual ones early. Under MIP-G (motivated information processing in groups), individual rewards trigger proself motives (more vetoing expected) and team rewards prosocial motives (less vetoing expected).

+ [ 02/DESIGN ]

Data and Method

German university lab: 80 teams (290 participants) randomly assigned to no reward, individual, team, or mixed rewards; two brainstorming rounds. Vetoing was coded from video, not self-reports.

+ [ 03/FINDINGS ]

Key Findings

  • Vetoing vs. mixed rewards: individual b = 0.99, team b = 0.86 (p < .01)
  • Individual rewards lowered creativity through vetoing (indirect effect −0.14)
  • Mixed rewards: highest information elaboration
+ [ 04/IMPLICATIONS ]

Implications for Team Design

Protect novel ideas in the convergence phase with explicit rules. Mixed rewards balance the costs of single-motive rewards. The paper airplane simulation (Variable Pay Plan Simulation) uses the same reward structures.
