What Is Organizational Culture?

WHAT IS ORGANIZATIONAL CULTURE?
[ DEFINITION ]
Organizational culture is a system of shared meaning held by members that distinguishes the organization from other organizations. It includes the organization's distinctive values, beliefs, and assumptions.
+[ 01/FOCUS ]

Shapes what gets attention

WHAT GETS ATTENTION

Shared values, beliefs, and assumptions determine where members focus: on detail or speed, on individual or team results.

+[ 02/EXPRESSION ]

Made visible

SYMBOLS AND STORIES

Culture itself is invisible. It shows through material symbols and company stories, such as office layout, founding stories, and the way meetings are run.

+[ 03/SHARED ]

Basis of shared understanding

SHARED UNDERSTANDING

Members share a similar sense of how things should be done, so much can be coordinated without written rules. Newcomers fit in by learning this understanding.

Culture Is a Descriptive Term

CULTURE IS A DESCRIPTIVE TERM
[ 2 CONCEPTS ]
+[ 01/DESCRIPTIVE ]

Organizational culture: descriptive

ORGANIZATIONAL CULTURE

Organizational culture reflects how members perceive the organization's characteristics, not whether they like them. Typical questions: Does the organization encourage teamwork? Reward innovation? Stifle initiative?

+[ 02/EVALUATIVE ]

Job satisfaction: evaluative

JOB SATISFACTION

Job satisfaction measures how members feel about the work environment, a judgment of liking or disliking. Two members can describe the same culture alike yet evaluate it in opposite ways: one satisfied, one not.

Culture asks what the organization is like; job satisfaction asks whether members like it

Dominant Culture and Subcultures

DOMINANT CULTURE AND SUBCULTURES
[ 3 TERMS ]
+[ 01/DOMINANT ]

Dominant culture

DOMINANT CULTURE

Most large organizations have a dominant culture that expresses the core values most members share and gives the organization its distinct personality. A company's culture usually refers to it.

+[ 02/CORE VALUES ]

Core values

CORE VALUES

The primary values accepted throughout the organization. They are the basis for judging cultural strength and the common ground that keeps subcultures within one organization.

+[ 03/SUBCULTURES ]

Subcultures

SUBCULTURES

Members of the same department or location who share problems and experiences develop subcultures. A subculture keeps the core values and adds values unique to its unit.

Strong Versus Weak Cultures

STRONG VERSUS WEAK CULTURES
[ STRENGTH ]
In a strong culture, the core values are widely shared and intensely held. The more members accept them and the greater their commitment, the stronger the culture and its influence on behavior.
+[ 01/AGREEMENT ]

High agreement

HIGH AGREEMENT

In a strong culture, members agree closely about what the organization values. In a weak culture, core values are less accepted and units disagree about what matters.

+[ 02/PERFORMANCE ]

Direct effect on performance

EFFECT ON PERFORMANCE

Shared goals build cohesiveness, loyalty, meaning, and organizational commitment, so strong cultures affect organizational performance more directly.

+[ 03/TURNOVER ]

Lower turnover

LOWER TURNOVER

Greater cohesiveness and commitment make members less inclined to leave. Strong cultures are associated with lower turnover intentions.

How Employees Learn Culture

HOW EMPLOYEES LEARN CULTURE
[ 4 CHANNELS ]
+[ 01/STORIES ]

Stories

STORIES

Stories typically concern founders, rule breaking, rags-to-riches successes, workforce reductions and restructuring, and reflections on past mistakes. They anchor the present in the past and explain current practices.

+[ 02/RITUALS ]

Rituals

RITUALS

Repetitive activities that express and reinforce the organization's key values, its most important goals, and its most important people, such as annual award ceremonies and set onboarding routines.

+[ 03/SYMBOLS ]

Symbols

MATERIAL SYMBOLS

Headquarters space and equipment, executive cars, and corporate jets are material symbols that convey who is important and what behavior is expected. Baidu's lounges, gyms, and nap rooms reflect a culture of trust in colleagues.

+[ 04/LANGUAGE ]

Language

LANGUAGE

Organizations and units use distinctive terms, acronyms, and jargon to identify members of a culture or subculture. Learning this language signals acceptance of the culture and helps preserve it.

How a Culture Begins

HOW A CULTURE BEGINS
[ FOUNDERS ]
An organization's culture traces back to its founders. Founders have a clear vision of the organization's future, and new organizations are usually small, so the vision is easier to instill in all members.
+[ 01/HIRE ]

Hiring like-minded people

HIRING

Founders tend to hire and keep employees who think and feel the way they do. Selection looks at ability and at similarity of values.

+[ 02/SOCIALIZE ]

Socializing

SOCIALIZING

Founders indoctrinate employees into their way of thinking and feeling; newcomers gradually adopt these views through daily interaction.

+[ 03/MODEL ]

Role modeling

ROLE MODELING

Founders' own behavior encourages employees to identify with them and internalize their beliefs, values, and assumptions.

Keeping a Culture Alive

KEEPING A CULTURE ALIVE
[ 3 FORCES ]
Once a culture is in place, organizational practices give members similar experiences and so maintain it. Three forces play a key role: selection practices, the actions of top management, and socialization.
+[ 01/SELECTION ]

Selection

SELECTION

Identify and hire candidates with the knowledge, skills, and abilities for the job, while screening for values that fit. Selection is two-way: either side can withdraw if the fit is poor.

+[ 02/TOP MANAGEMENT ]

Top management

TOP MANAGEMENT

Senior executives' words and deeds set norms that filter through the organization: whether risk taking is encouraged, how much freedom employees get, and what earns raises and promotions.

+[ 03/SOCIALIZATION ]

Socialization

SOCIALIZATION

The process that helps new employees acquire social knowledge and needed skills and adapt to the culture. Onboarding and training are the main methods.

Entry Socialization Options

ENTRY SOCIALIZATION OPTIONS
[ 5 CHOICES ]
Formal vs. informalFORMAL/INFORMAL
Formal socialization separates newcomers from the ongoing work setting and marks them as new, as in orientation and training programs. Informal socialization puts newcomers straight into the job with little special attention.
Individual vs. collectiveINDIVIDUAL/COLLECTIVE
Newcomers can be socialized individually, as in many professional offices, or grouped together for identical experiences, as in military boot camp.
Fixed vs. variableFIXED/VARIABLE
The time schedule for moving from outsider to insider. Fixed schedules have standardized transition stages, such as rotational training and probation; variable schedules give no advance notice, and people are promoted when ready.
Serial vs. randomSERIAL/RANDOM
Serial socialization uses role models who train and encourage newcomers, as in apprenticeship and mentoring programs. Random socialization deliberately withholds role models, leaving newcomers to work things out.
Investiture vs. divestitureINVESTITURE/DIVESTITURE
Investiture socialization affirms and supports newcomers' existing qualities and qualifications. Divestiture socialization tries to strip away certain characteristics, as in fraternity and sorority pledging.
Formal, collective, fixed, serial, and divestiture socialization makes behavior more uniform and predictable; the reverse keeps individual differences

Defines boundaries

BOUNDARY-DEFINING

SOCIAL STABILITY

Sense-making and control

SENSE-MAKING AND CONTROL

Culture Creates Climate

CULTURE CREATES CLIMATE
[ CLIMATE ]
Organizational climate refers to members' shared perceptions of the organization and work environment; within culture it plays a role similar to team spirit.
+[ 01/OUTCOMES ]

Climate and outcomes

CLIMATE AND OUTCOMES

A positive overall climate is linked to higher customer satisfaction and financial performance. Climate has many dimensions, such as safety, justice, diversity, and customer service, each shaping related behavior.

+[ 02/INTERACTION ]

Interacting climates

CLIMATES INTERACT

Different climates interact. For example, a climate of empowerment combined with a climate of personal accountability leads to higher performance.

+[ 03/HABITS ]

Climate shapes habits

CLIMATE SHAPES HABITS

Climate also shapes the habits people form. In a positive safety climate, members wear safety gear and follow safety procedures even if they are not personally focused on safety.

What Culture Brings

CULTURE AS AN ASSET
[ 4 FACETS ]
+[ 01/ASSET ]

Culture as an asset

CULTURE AS AN ASSET

Culture can raise an organization's profits in many ways. Google, Adobe, and Samsung have been named best places to work because of their corporate cultures.

+[ 02/ETHICS ]

Ethical dimension

ETHICAL DIMENSION

Organizational culture is never neutral in its ethical orientation, even when an organization does not state ethical goals. Ethical culture is the shared understanding of right and wrong workplace behavior, formed over time.

+[ 03/SUSTAINABILITY ]

Sustainability

SUSTAINABILITY

Sustainability means a practice can be maintained over a long period because the tools or structures that support it are not damaged by the process. Sustainable management is not always purely altruistic; it requires a long-term culture and a matching climate.

+[ 04/INNOVATION ]

Innovative culture

INNOVATIVE CULTURE

The most innovative companies tend to have open, unconventional, collaborative, visionary, accelerating cultures. Samsung's Creative Lab mimics a start-up culture, and employees can spend up to a year on innovation projects.

Supplementary Research: How Executives View Corporate Culture

CORPORATE CULTURE: EVIDENCE FROM THE FIELD ── GRAHAM ET AL.
[ JFE 2022 ]
N = 1,348
In a survey of 1,348 North American executives, 92% said improving corporate culture would raise firm value and 84% said their firm's culture needed improvement. Executives linked culture to value creation, ethical choices, and innovation.
+[ 01/DESIGN ]

Research question and data

  • Culture is hard to quantify, so the study asked executives directly what it is, how it works, and whether it affects firm value
  • Survey of 1,348 North American executives plus interviews at large firms; results matched external public data
  • Two levels: the values a firm states and the norms of daily practice
[ 02/VALUE ]

Value creation: productivity and mergers

  • Over half ranked culture among the top three drivers of firm value
  • In mergers, 54% would walk away from a target with poor cultural fit
  • About one third would cut their offer by 10% to 30% for poor cultural fit
[ 03/ETHICS ]

Ethical choices: compliance and short-termism

  • 85% said poorly implemented culture makes unethical or illegal behavior more likely
  • Short-termism: 41% would pick a project with higher short-term earnings but lower net present value
  • Of executives choosing long-term projects, 80% said culture influenced the choice
[ 04/INNOVATION ]

Innovation and cultural barriers

  • Culture affects creativity and employees' willingness to take appropriate risks
  • 69% said their firm underinvests in culture
  • Other barriers: too little leader attention; rewards inconsistent with stated values
Over 90% of executives see culture as affecting firm value; daily norms matter as much as stated values
Graham, J. R., Grennan, J., Harvey, C. R., & Rajgopal, S. (2022). Corporate culture: Evidence from the field. Journal of Financial Economics, 146(2), 552–593. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2022.07.008

Liabilities of Organizational Culture

CULTURE AS A LIABILITY
[ 4 RISKS ]
+[ 01/STAGNATION ]

Institutionalization

STAGNATION AND ENTRENCHMENT

Once culture is institutionalized, the organization is valued for itself rather than for its goods or services. Established ways of behaving become taken for granted and rarely questioned, so innovation and adaptation stall.

+[ 02/RIGIDITY ]

Uniformity and rigidity

UNIFORMITY AND RIGIDITY

When the environment changes rapidly, shared values that once worked may no longer fit, and a strong culture becomes a barrier to change. Strong cultures also press new members to conform, weakening the advantages of diverse backgrounds.

+[ 03/TOXICITY ]

Toxicity and dysfunctions

TOXICITY AND DYSFUNCTIONS

A culture can also form consensus around negative values, such as tolerating bullying, mismanagement, or misconduct. Such cultures lower satisfaction, raise turnover, and spread through the organization.

+[ 04/CLASHES ]

Culture clash

CULTURE CLASHES

In mergers, cultural compatibility matters as much as financial and product synergies. Many mergers fall short of expectations because of culture clash.

Developing a Positive Organizational Culture

A POSITIVE ORGANIZATIONAL CULTURE
[ 3 PRACTICES + LIMITS ]
+[ 01/STRENGTHS ]

Building on employee strengths

BUILDING ON EMPLOYEE STRENGTHS

Help employees discover their strengths and assign work that uses them.

+[ 02/REWARDS ]

Rewarding more than punishing

REWARDING MORE THAN PUNISHING

Use more rewards and fewer punishments. Beyond pay and promotions, timely, specific praise is cheap and effective, yet many managers rarely give it.

+[ 03/GROWTH ]

Emphasizing vitality and growth

ENCOURAGING VITALITY AND GROWTH

Treat employees as people who can grow, and care about their careers and personal development; value what employees can do for the organization and what the organization can do for them.

+[ 04/LIMITS ]

Limits of positive culture

LIMITS OF POSITIVE CULTURE

A positive culture is not a cure-all. Countries differ in how much they value positive expression, and too much emphasis on positivity can suppress legitimate negative emotions. Some negative emotion is natural; positive does not mean perfect.

Creating an Ethical Culture

DEVELOPING AN ETHICAL CULTURE
[ 5 PRACTICES ]
Role modelingROLE MODEL
Employees judge by the behavior of top management. Ethical behavior by managers sends the clearest message.
Communicate ethical expectationsEXPECTATIONS
Create and publish a code of ethics stating the organization's primary values and the ethical rules employees are expected to follow.
Provide ethics trainingETHICS TRAINING
Use seminars, workshops, and training programs to reinforce standards of conduct, clarify what practices are acceptable, and address possible ethical dilemmas.
Visibly reward and punishREWARD AND PUNISH
Appraise managers on how their decisions measure against the code of ethics. Visibly reward ethical acts and conspicuously punish unethical ones.

Create formal channels where employees can discuss ethical dilemmas and report unethical behavior without fear of reprisal, and protect whistleblowers.

Creating an Innovative Culture

DEVELOPING AN INNOVATIVE CULTURE
[ 3 CONDITIONS ]
Innovation is a specific kind of change: a new idea applied to initiating or improving a product, process, or service.
+[ 01/SOURCES ]

Sources of innovation

SOURCES OF INNOVATION

Structural variables are the most studied source. Organic structures, long managerial tenure, slack resources, and frequent interunit communication all relate positively to innovation.

+[ 02/CONTEXT ]

Context and innovation

CONTEXT AND INNOVATION

Innovative organizations encourage experimentation and reward both successes and failures. Members who do not fear punishment for mistakes more readily propose and try new ideas.

+[ 03/CHAMPIONS ]

Idea champions

IDEA CHAMPIONS

Idea champions actively and enthusiastically promote ideas, build support, overcome resistance, and see ideas implemented. They tend to be confident, persistent, energetic risk takers.

Why Serendipity Matters

WHY SERENDIPITY MATTERS
[ BUSCH 2024 ]
Serendipity is an unexpected, valuable discovery made at an unplanned moment through one's own decisions and actions. Busch (2024) notes that serendipity plays an important role in both individual and organizational success.
+[ 01/INNOVATION ]

Many innovations stem from serendipity

SOURCE OF INNOVATION

Many innovations and inventions trace back to serendipity, such as Viagra, Velcro, and a potato-washing machine. Many organizations credit their success to it, as it brings new products, services, insights, and markets.

+[ 02/AGENCY ]

Unlike luck, it requires action

NOT THE SAME AS LUCK

Good luck can fall to passive people; a serendipitous discovery requires active exploration plus alertness and flexibility. Because it involves action, individuals and organizations can influence the outcome.

+[ 03/GAP ]

Important but lacking usable theory

AN OVERLOOKED TOPIC

Managers tend to credit success to planned strategy and rarely admit serendipity's role. Research is scattered and concepts are blurred; over half of the serendipity studies in top management journals appeared in the five years before the review.

Busch, C. (2024). Towards a theory of serendipity: A systematic review and conceptualization. Journal of Management Studies, 61(3), 1110–1151. https://doi.org/10.1111/joms.12890

Supplementary Research: How Organizations Cultivate Serendipity

SERENDIPITY IN ORGANIZATIONS ── BUSCH (2024)
[ JMS 2024 ]
SYSTEMATIC REVIEW
Serendipity is often treated as a synonym for luck. Reviewing the management literature, Busch shows they differ: luck needs only surprise and value, while serendipity also needs action. This is why luck cannot be managed, while organizations can design for serendipity.
+[ 01/CONCEPT ]

Where serendipity and luck divide

  • Three conditions must all be present: action, surprise, and value
  • Luck has only surprise and value; lacking action, it offers nothing to intervene on. Serendipity involves action, so it can be designed
  • Two stages: notice unexpected cues and link unrelated ideas; then the organization decides which can be realized
[ 02/INDIVIDUAL ]

What individuals can practice

  • Noticing: stay alert, curious, and intuitive about unplanned information rather than dismissing it as noise
  • Connecting: use insight, creativity, and analogy to link two seemingly unrelated things
  • Suppress functional fixedness (seeing only existing uses) and self-censorship (withholding unfinished ideas)
[ 03/ORGANIZATION ]

What organizations can do

  • Social integration: use space and systems for cross-unit contact. Pixar centered its atrium, café, and mailboxes so separate teams had to pass one another
  • Resource integration: evaluation and investment mechanisms screen and fund unexpected ideas; existing infrastructure scales them
  • Broader problem framing: a narrow frame leaves fewer things to connect. Frame the problem broadly first, then narrow it
[ 04/PRECONDITION ]

Preconditions and limits

  • Psychological safety is a precondition: potential forms only when employees dare to voice half-formed ideas
  • Serendipity is probabilistic: its likelihood can be raised, but outcomes cannot be guaranteed
  • Noticed opportunities may still go unrealized due to resource limits or power relations; power also shapes what counts as a problem and which ideas are chosen
Serendipity needs action, surprise, and value; luck lacks action, so what organizations can influence is serendipity
Busch, C. (2024). Towards a theory of serendipity: A systematic review and conceptualization. Journal of Management Studies, 61(3), 1110–1151. https://doi.org/10.1111/joms.12890

Resistance to Change

RESISTANCE TO CHANGE
[ 3 POINTS ]
+[ 01/THREAT ]

Change seen as a threat

CHANGE AS THREAT

Our egos are fragile, and we often see change as threatening. Even when data show change is needed, most people still look for information that supports the status quo.

+[ 02/VALUE ]

Resistance can be positive

RESISTANCE CAN BE POSITIVE

If resistance leads to open discussion or debate, change agents can explain their reasons and revise the plan. Responsive resistance is easier to handle than apathy or silence.

+[ 03/FORMS ]

Forms of resistance

FORMS OF RESISTANCE

Resistance can be overt or implicit, immediate or deferred. Overt, immediate resistance (complaints, slowdowns) is easiest to handle; implicit or deferred resistance (lost loyalty, absenteeism) is harder to detect.

Sources of Resistance: Individual

SOURCES OF RESISTANCE ── INDIVIDUAL
[ 5 SOURCES ]
HabitHABIT
To cope with life's complexity, we rely on habits. When change comes, those habits become a source of resistance.
SecuritySECURITY
People with a high need for security are likely to see change as threatening and resist it.
Economic factorsECONOMIC FACTORS
When job tasks or routines change, employees worry they cannot adapt or perform well and will lose income, especially when pay is closely tied to productivity.
Fear of the unknownFEAR OF THE UNKNOWN
Change replaces the known with ambiguity and uncertainty.
Selective information processingINFO PROCESSING
People process information selectively to keep their perceptions intact: they hear what they want to hear and ignore information that challenges their views.

Sources of Resistance: Organizational

SOURCES OF RESISTANCE ── ORGANIZATIONAL
[ 5 SOURCES ]
Structural inertiaSTRUCTURAL INERTIA
Organizations have built-in mechanisms for stability, such as selection processes and formal regulations. When change comes, this structural inertia acts as a counterbalance.
Limited focus of changeLIMITED FOCUS
Organizations consist of interdependent subsystems; changing one affects the others. Limited changes in one part are often nullified by the larger system.
Group inertiaGROUP INERTIA
Even when individuals want to change their behavior, group norms may act as a constraint.
Threat to expertiseTHREAT TO EXPERTISE
Organizational change may threaten the expertise of specialized groups.
Threat to established powerTHREAT TO POWER
Redistributing decision-making authority can threaten long-established power relationships.

Overcoming Resistance to Change (1)

OVERCOMING RESISTANCE TO CHANGE
[ 01-04/08 ]
+[ 01/COMMUNICATION ]

Communication

COMMUNICATION

Explaining the rationale for change reduces resistance caused by misinformation or poor communication. Thorough communication also helps convince members that change is necessary.

+[ 02/PARTICIPATION ]

Participation

PARTICIPATION

It is difficult to resist a decision one helped make. When participants have expertise to contribute, participation lowers resistance, builds commitment, and improves decision quality; the costs are time and possibly a poorer solution.

+[ 03/SUPPORT ]

Building support and commitment

BUILDING SUPPORT AND COMMITMENT

When fear and anxiety are high, counseling, new-skills training, or a short paid leave can help employees adjust. The higher their affective commitment to the organization, the more they support change.

+[ 04/RELATIONSHIPS ]

Developing positive relationships

DEVELOPING POSITIVE RELATIONSHIPS

Employees accept change more readily when they trust the managers implementing it. Those with good supervisor relationships who see the work setting as supporting their development view change more positively.

Overcoming Resistance to Change (2)

OVERCOMING RESISTANCE TO CHANGE
[ 05-08/08 ]
+[ 05/FAIRNESS ]

Implementing changes fairly

IMPLEMENTING CHANGES FAIRLY

When employees see a change's outcomes as negative, procedural fairness is especially important. Employees should understand the reasons for the change and see it implemented consistently and fairly.

+[ 06/MANIPULATION ]

Manipulation and cooptation

MANIPULATION AND COOPTATION

Manipulation is covert influence, such as distorting facts to make change look attractive or withholding information. Cooptation brings resistance leaders into decisions in exchange for support. Both are cheap, but if discovered, the change agent's credibility suffers.

+[ 07/SELECTION ]

Selecting people who accept change

SELECTING PEOPLE WHO ACCEPT CHANGE

The ability to accept and adapt to change is related to personality. People open to experience, willing to take risks, and flexible in behavior handle change better.

+[ 08/COERCION ]

Coercion

COERCION

Direct threats or force applied to resisters, such as threatened transfers, loss of promotion, or negative evaluations. Its pros and cons resemble those of manipulation and cooptation.

The Politics of Change

THE POLITICS OF CHANGE
[ WHO LEADS CHANGE ]
Change usually disturbs the status quo and poses threats, so political behavior is almost inevitable. Politically, the impetus for change is more likely to come from people with less stake in the status quo.
+[ 01/OUTSIDE ]

Outside consultants

OUTSIDE CONSULTANTS

Less tied to the organization's existing interests and relationships, they can point out problems from an outside view.

+[ 02/NEW ]

New employees

NEW EMPLOYEES

With less invested in the status quo, they are more willing to question established practices.

+[ 03/PERIPHERAL ]

Managers outside the power core

MANAGERS OUTSIDE THE POWER CENTER

Managers slightly removed from the main power structure lose less by changing the status quo. Executives who have spent whole careers in one organization are often a major source of resistance.

Supplementary Research: Organizational Change Evokes Nostalgia

ORGANIZATIONAL CHANGE AND ORGANIZATIONAL NOSTALGIA ── VAN DIJKE ET AL.
[ JAP 2025 ]
9 STUDIES
Organizational change is usually thought to trigger negative reactions. Nine studies found that perceived organizational change evokes not only change apprehension but also nostalgia for past organizational life, and nostalgia raises organizational commitment and organizational citizenship behavior.
+[ 01/PUZZLE ]

Research question

  • Change is thought to provoke negative reactions, yet it is constant across organizations and does less damage than expected
  • Two opposing pathways are proposed to explain this gap
  • Organizational citizenship behavior (OCB): discretionary behavior beyond formal duties that benefits the organization
[ 02/DESIGN ]

Data and method

  • Nine studies, most preregistered
  • Seven experiments manipulated perceived social or organizational change, or directly induced organizational nostalgia
  • Two surveys tested the full model
[ 03/FINDINGS ]

Key findings

  • Pathway 1: perceived change raises change apprehension, lowering commitment and OCB
  • Pathway 2: perceived change evokes organizational nostalgia, raising commitment and OCB
  • Participants induced to feel nostalgia were more committed after the change and more willing to defend it
[ 04/IMPLICATIONS ]

Implications for managing change

  • Employee reactions to change are mixed; apprehension and nostalgia can coexist
  • Missing the past and resisting change differ; here nostalgia went with pro-organizational behavior
  • Further thought: when communicating change, state what will continue as well as what will change
Perceived change triggers both apprehension and nostalgia, which act on organizational commitment in opposite directions
Van Dijke, M., Guo, Y., Wildschut, T., & Sedikides, C. (2025). Perceived organizational change strengthens organizational commitment and organizational citizenship behavior via increased organizational nostalgia. Journal of Applied Psychology, 110(1), 89–106. https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0001221

Lewin's Three-Step Model

LEWIN'S THREE-STEP MODEL
[ 3 STEPS ]
Kurt Lewin argued that successful organizational change should follow three steps: unfreezing the status quo, movement to a desired end state, and refreezing the new change to make it permanent.
+[ 01/UNFREEZING ]

Unfreezing

UNFREEZING

The status quo is an equilibrium. Leaving it requires overcoming individual resistance and group conformity pressure, by increasing driving forces, decreasing restraining forces, or both.

+[ 02/MOVEMENT ]

Movement

MOVEMENT

After unfreezing, change is put into practice. Momentum must be kept once change begins; organizations that move through this stage quickly outperform those that change gradually.

+[ 03/REFREEZING ]

Refreezing

REFREEZING

The new situation must be stabilized after implementation, or the change will likely be short-lived as employees revert to the old equilibrium. Driving and restraining forces are rebalanced, for example by adjusting rewards.

Action Research

ACTION RESEARCH
[ 5 STEPS ]
Action research is a change process based on the systematic collection of data and selection of a change action based on what the analyzed data indicate.
DiagnosisDIAGNOSIS
The change agent gathers information from members about problems, concerns, and needed changes, by interviewing, reviewing records, and listening to employees' concerns.
AnalysisANALYSIS
The information gathered is organized into primary concerns, problem areas, and possible actions.
FeedbackFEEDBACK
Findings from diagnosis and analysis are shared with employees, who develop action plans with the change agent's help.
ActionACTION
Employees and the change agent carry out the plans to correct the problems identified.
EvaluationEVALUATION
The initial data serve as a benchmark for comparing and evaluating the effectiveness of the change.
Two benefits: it is problem focused, understanding the problem before choosing an action; employees take part throughout, so resistance is lower

Organizational Development

ORGANIZATIONAL DEVELOPMENT
[ 4 TECHNIQUES ]
Organizational development (OD) is a collection of planned change methods that seek to improve organizational effectiveness and employee well-being. OD values human growth, collaboration and participation, and a spirit of inquiry.
+[ 01/PROCESS ]

Process consultation

PROCESS CONSULTATION

An outside consultant helps managers perceive, understand, and act on process events such as workflow, informal relationships among members, and communication channels. The consultant guides; managers find the solutions themselves.

+[ 02/TEAM ]

Team building

TEAM BUILDING

Highly interactive group activities build trust and openness among members, typically covering goal setting, interpersonal relations, role and responsibility analysis, and team process analysis.

+[ 03/INTERGROUP ]

Intergroup development

INTERGROUP DEVELOPMENT

Seeks to change groups' attitudes, stereotypes, and perceptions of each other. Typically each group lists its views of itself and of the other group, then the groups discuss similarities, differences, and the causes of disparities.

+[ 04/APPRECIATIVE ]

Appreciative inquiry

APPRECIATIVE INQUIRY

Starts from the organization's unique qualities and strengths and builds on them to improve performance, unlike most approaches that start from problems. Four steps: discovery, dreaming, design, and destiny.

The Paradox of Change

THE CHANGE PARADOX
[ PARADOX THEORY ]
Paradox theory states that the key paradox in management is that there is no final optimal state for an organization.
+[ 01/BALANCE ]

Constant balancing

ONGOING BALANCE

In a paradoxical situation, managers must constantly balance tensions between alternative courses of action, most of which stem from limited resources. Day-to-day operations must continue while change is under way.

+[ 02/HOLISTIC ]

Holistic thinking

HOLISTIC THINKING

Research shows that managers who think holistically and recognize the importance of balancing paradoxical factors are more effective, especially at eliciting adaptive and creative behavior from subordinates.

Supplementary Research: Employee Reactions to Planned Culture Change

EMPLOYEE REACTIONS TO PLANNED CULTURE CHANGE ── TASOULIS ET AL.
[ HUM RELAT 2024 ]
59 INTERVIEWS
UK retailer John Lewis Partnership moved its culture from civil-service-style security toward high performance. From 59 interviews, the study identified five pathways of employee reaction, three positive and two negative; the positive pathways shared perceived organizational support and a favorable view of empowerment.
+[ 01/QUESTION ]

Research question

  • Can organizational culture be changed deliberately, and what paths lead to success or failure?
  • Configurational view: how combinations of conditions, not single factors, shape reactions
  • Case: an employee-owned retailer moving from a paternalistic, bureaucratic, secure culture to high support and high performance
[ 02/DESIGN ]

Data and method

  • 59 semi-structured interviews at two department stores, analysis focused on 32 non-managerial staff, plus meeting observations and company documents
  • Fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA) identified condition combinations
  • Eight conditions: psychological contract, perceived organizational and supervisor support, empowerment, training, employee representation, tenure, work area
[ 03/FINDINGS ]

Key findings

  • Positive: comparative (short tenure, better than past employers), exculpatory (change forced by the market), tolerant (long tenure, accepting despite doubts)
  • Negative: disillusioned (long tenure, psychological contract breached), alienated (lacking organizational and supervisor support)
  • Perceived organizational support and empowerment are the core conditions of positive pathways
[ 04/IMPLICATIONS ]

Implications for culture change

  • The same change draws different reactions from different employee groups, so approaches should be tailored
  • More than one combination leads to success: weak supervisor support or participation can be offset by other conditions
  • Limitations: one UK organization; small, positively skewed sample
Culture change succeeds or fails on combinations of conditions; perceived organizational support and empowerment are the common core of positive reactions
Tasoulis, K., Pappas, I. O., Vlachos, P., & Oruh, E. S. (2024). Employee reactions to planned organizational culture change: A configurational perspective. Human Relations, 77(9), 1272–1305. https://doi.org/10.1177/00187267231183305

Implications for Managers: Culture

IMPLICATIONS FOR MANAGERS ── CULTURE
[ 4 POINTS ]
+[ 01 ]

Culture is fairly stable in the short run

Organizational culture is hard to change in the short term. Culture change needs a long-term plan that works through selection, top management actions, and socialization.

+[ 02 ]

Match culture emphasis to goals

Strengthen a clan culture for innovation; a market culture for customer satisfaction and quality; an adhocracy culture for employee attitudes and performance; a hierarchy culture when profit and revenue matter most.

+[ 03 ]

Watch for subcultures

Beneath a seemingly strong dominant culture, departments, teams, or sites may have subcultures. When one appears, first understand why it split off, then decide how to meet its members' needs.

+[ 04 ]

Use transmission and climate

Stories, rituals, material symbols, and language are powerful ways to build and sustain culture. Values and assumptions are fairly fixed, so leaders can start by building a climate that puts the values into practice.

Implications for Managers: Change

IMPLICATIONS FOR MANAGERS ── CHANGE
[ 4 POINTS ]
+[ 01 ]

Avoid culture traps

If an organization cannot adapt to change, institutionalization, uniformity, rigidity, and toxic culture can all lead to failure.

+[ 02 ]

Positive and ethical cultures

Develop employee strengths, reward more than punish, and encourage growth; leaders model ethics, state ethical expectations, provide training, reward and punish clearly, and protect whistleblowers.

+[ 03 ]

Innovative culture

Build structures and environments that foster creativity, and make sure someone actively promotes new ideas so they spread through the organization.

+[ 04 ]

Change is hard; methods vary

Organizational change is very difficult and likely to fail, which does not mean the effort lacks value. Depending on what is to be changed, different methods and tools apply, such as OD techniques.
